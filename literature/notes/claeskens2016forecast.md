# claeskens2016forecast

*   **Citation:** Claeskens, G., Magnus, J. R., Vasnev, A. L., & Wang, W. (2016). The forecast combination puzzle: A simple theoretical explanation. International Journal of Forecasting 32(3), 754-762.
*   **Identifier:** DOI 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2015.12.005; OpenAlex W3123965474 — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijforecast.2015.12.005
*   **Retrieved:** 2026-11-09
*   **Access:** full text (author manuscript from KU Leuven Lirias, https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/26ea3ca0-1488-49de-ba6b-4a8cd5674f35; `sources/claeskens2016forecast.pdf`, `sources/claeskens2016forecast.txt`)
*   **Type:** peer-reviewed article (quotes from the author manuscript)
*   **Relevance:** Theory that estimated "optimal" weights become random, biased and higher-variance, with no guarantee of beating equal weights.

## Contribution

*   **Research problem:** Why combinations with estimated optimal weights often perform poorly.
*   **Method:** Derive combination properties treating weights as random (estimated).
*   **Data or materials:** Numerical illustration.
*   **Main result:** Estimated-weight combinations can be biased with larger variance; no guarantee of beating equal weights.

## Quotes

1.  "This paper offers a theoretical explanation for the stylized fact that forecast combinations with estimated optimal weights often perform poorly in applications." (abstract)
    *   Supports: aim of the paper.
2.  "If the fact that the weights are random rather than fixed is taken into account during the optimality derivation, then the forecast combination will be biased (even when the original forecasts are unbiased) and its variance is larger than in the fixed-weights case." (abstract)
    *   Supports: estimated weights introduce bias and extra variance.
3.  "In particular, there is no guarantee that the ‘optimal’ forecast combination will be better than the equal-weights case or even improve on the original forecasts." (abstract)
    *   Supports: estimated-weight combinations need not beat equal weights.

## Not Covered

*   Empirical climate evidence; absolute-error loss.
