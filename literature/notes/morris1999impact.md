# morris1999impact

*   **Citation:** Morris, M. (1999, approx.; PDF created 1999-09-02). The Impact of Temperature Trends on Short-Term Energy Demand. U.S. Energy Information Administration, STEO special article.
*   **Identifier:** URL — https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/special/pdf/temptrnd.pdf
*   **Retrieved:** 2026-11-09
*   **Access:** full text (`sources/morris1999impact.pdf`, `sources/morris1999impact.txt`)
*   **Type:** report
*   **Relevance:** Early agency analysis that 30-year norms used for degree-day-based energy projections miss warming trends.

## Contribution

*   **Research problem:** Whether 30-year weather norms used for energy demand projections reflect recent warming.
*   **Method:** Uses Livezey and Smith (1999) trend estimates to adjust weather premises.
*   **Data or materials:** Lower-48 U.S. temperature (as summarized from Livezey and Smith).
*   **Main result:** 30-year norms do not reflect the warming trend.

## Quotes

1.  "The analysis shows that the 30-year norms--the basis of weather-related energy demand projections--do not reflect the warming trend or its regional and seasonal patterns." (introduction)
    *   Supports: EIA judged 30-yr norms not to reflect the warming trend.
2.  "The concentration of the warming trend during the winter season results in a reduction of projected space-heating requirements exceeding increases in summer cooling demand that also result from the same trend." (introduction)
    *   Supports: trend-adjusted premises raise cooling demand by less than they cut heating demand.
3.  "LS estimated the average national warming trend to be 0.015 degrees Fahrenheit per year." (section "The Livezey and Smith Findings")
    *   Supports: the trend value EIA took from Livezey and Smith (1999).
