# vose2017temperature

*   **Citation:** Vose, R. S., Easterling, D. R., Kunkel, K. E., LeGrande, A. N., & Wehner, M. F. (2017). Temperature changes in the United States. In: Climate Science Special Report: Fourth National Climate Assessment, Volume I (Wuebbles, D. J., et al., eds.). U.S. Global Change Research Program, Washington, DC, pp. 185-206.
*   **Identifier:** DOI 10.7930/J0N29V45; NASA NTRS 20180001314 — https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20180001314
*   **Retrieved:** 2026-11-09
*   **Access:** full text (NASA NTRS copy, `sources/vose2017temperature.pdf`, `sources/vose2017temperature.txt`; science2017.globalchange.gov and its CloudFront copy did not respond)
*   **Type:** report (assessment chapter)
*   **Relevance:** Observed U.S. trends in annual mean temperature and in the warmest daily temperature of the year (annual max Tmax), including the eastern "warming hole" and the 1930s peak.

## Contribution

*   **Research problem:** Assess observed and projected U.S. temperature change.
*   **Method:** Assessment of station, satellite and proxy evidence; CMIP5 projections.
*   **Data or materials:** GHCN-Daily long-term stations and other datasets.
*   **Main result:** CONUS annual mean warmed 1.2°F (1986-2016 vs 1901-1960); warmest day of the year decreased in most locations east of the Rockies over the century.

## Quotes

1.  "Annual average temperature over the contiguous United States has increased by 1.2°F (0.7°C) for the period 1986–2016 relative to 1901–1960 and by 1.8°F (1.0°C) based on a linear regression for the period 1895–2016 (very high confidence)." (Key Finding 1)
    *   Supports: magnitude of observed CONUS annual mean warming.
2.  "The Dust Bowl era of the 1930s remains the peak period for extreme heat." (Key Finding 2)
    *   Supports: the 1930s are the peak for U.S. extreme heat.
3.  "the warmest daily temperature of the year increased in some parts of the West over the past century (Figure 6.3), but there were decreases in almost all locations east of the Rocky Mountains." (section 6.1.2 Temperature Extremes, p. 190)
    *   Supports: annual max Tmax trends are mixed: up in parts of the West, down in most of the East.
4.  "The decreases in the eastern half of Nation, particularly in the Great Plains, are mainly tied to the unprecedented summer heat of the 1930s Dust Bowl era, which was exacerbated by land-surface feedbacks driven by springtime" (section 6.1.2, p. 190; the sentence continues on p. 191 after a figure: "precipitation deficits and land mismanagement.")
    *   Supports: the eastern decrease in the warmest day reflects 1930s heat amplified by precipitation-deficit land-surface feedbacks.
5.  "Since the mid-1960s, there has been only a very slight increase in the warmest daily temperature of the year (amidst large interannual variability)." (section 6.1.2, p. 191)
    *   Supports: little trend in annual max Tmax since the mid-1960s, with large year-to-year variability.
6.  "However, anthropogenic aerosol forcing may also have reduced summer temperatures in the Northeast and Southeast from the early 1950s to the mid-1970s,19 and agricultural intensification may have suppressed the hottest extremes in the Midwest.20" (section 6.1.2, p. 191; superscript reference numbers as extracted)
    *   Supports: proposed causes for suppressed hot extremes in the East and Midwest.
7.  "of this “warming hole,” or lack of a long-term warming trend, remains uncertain, though it is likely a combination of natural and human causes." (section 6.2.1 Average Temperatures; the sentence begins before an intervening figure)
    *   Supports: warming-hole cause uncertain, likely mixed natural and human.
